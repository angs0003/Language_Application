# Research: effective, evidence-based ways to learn a language

Date: 2026-11-14. Scope: learning methods compared with Language Steps. No code was changed.
Method note: the sources below were found with web search. Where only secondary pages (summaries, blogs) were retrieved, the finding says so and the confidence is lower. Numbers marked "unverified" were not checked in the primary paper.

## 1. Answer in 3 lines

1. The strongest evidence supports retrieval practice (testing yourself) and spaced practice; the app already does both. Its weaknesses are the fixed interval table (not adaptive) and almost no training for tones and pitch accent.
2. The best next gains are: (a) schedule reviews by difficulty and by word, (b) add listening-discrimination practice for Chinese tones and Japanese pitch accent, (c) add more easy, graded listening and reading with known words.
3. Several popular claims are weak: learning styles, kanji mnemonics as a general rule, the "85 percent rule" for students, "optimal gap is 10 to 20 percent", and streak mechanics. The app is correct to avoid streaks. Evidence for ADHD and autism specific tactics is thin; plain predictability and low sensory load are low risk.

## 2. Findings

Each finding has: source, evidence kind, confidence.

### 2.1 Spaced repetition (timing)

1. Practice spread over time beats massed practice, for all ages and materials tested. Source: [Dunlosky et al. 2013, review of 10 techniques (summary page)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html). Evidence: review. Confidence: about 90%. Only a summary page was read, not the full paper.
2. The best gap between study sessions depends on how long you need to remember: the longer the retention goal, the longer the best gap. Too short and too long gaps are both worse than the best one. Source: [Cepeda et al. 2006, 317 experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/) and [Cepeda et al. 2008, 1,350 people](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1766/). Evidence: meta-analysis and large controlled study. Confidence: about 85%.
3. Weak claim: "the optimal gap is 10 to 20 percent of the retention time". The 2008 abstract says the optimal proportion declines as the test delay grows (about 20 to 40 percent for a one-week test). The fixed 10 to 20 percent rule appears only on secondary blogs. Confidence that the fixed rule is oversimplified: about 75%.
4. Expanding intervals (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 days, like the app) are a reasonable heuristic. The meta-analysis supports longer gaps for longer goals; it does not show that exact doubling is best. Source: same as item 2. Evidence: meta-analysis (indirect). Confidence: about 70%.
5. Adaptive schedulers predict recall better than fixed tables. Half-life regression, trained on Duolingo learner data, had at least 45 percent lower error in predicting recall than baselines (Leitner and others). Sources: [Settles and Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/naacl24-info/P16-1174), [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn), [Tabibian et al. 2019, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6410796). Evidence: controlled model comparison on company data (peer reviewed, but the data is from the vendor's users). Confidence: about 75% that adaptive beats fixed for prediction; about 55% that it gives a large gain in what people learn (the "69 percent" claim for Tabibian is unverified).
6. FSRS (used in Anki) lets the user choose a target retention (90 percent suggested) and models memory per item. Anki documentation says higher retention means more reviews per day. Sources: [Anki FAQ](https://faqs.ankiweb.net/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm), [FSRS4Anki documentation](https://docsearch.algolia.com/mcp/docs/repo/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki). Evidence: official documentation. Confidence: about 90% for the description.
7. Claims that FSRS beats SM-2 for 99.6 percent of users and cuts reviews by 20 to 30 percent came from secondary sites; the primary benchmark repository (open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark) was not retrieved. Source: [secondary summary](https://dev.to/palachdigital_20fd679f5fe/why-fsrs-is-better-than-sm-2-2m17). Evidence: vendor and community claim. Confidence: about 50%. Note that the benchmark measures prediction accuracy, not learning outcomes. No controlled trial of learning outcome FSRS versus SM-2 was found.

### 2.2 Retrieval practice and the testing effect

1. Recalling beats restudying. Rowland 2014: g = 0.50 (61 studies); effect larger when the final test is one day or more later (g = 0.69) and when practice is recall rather than recognition. Adesope et al. 2017: g about 0.61 (118 articles; the figure is from a secondary page). Sources: [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf), [Adesope et al. 2017](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842). Evidence: meta-analyses. Confidence: about 90%.
2. Feedback after the test and typing or saying the answer (recall) give more than choosing from options. Source: Rowland 2014 (above) and the Dunlosky summary. Evidence: meta-analysis. Confidence: about 80%.
3. Rereading, highlighting and summarising have low utility; the keyword mnemonic and imagery were also rated low for general learning. Source: [Dunlosky et al. 2013 summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html). Evidence: review. Confidence: about 85%.

### 2.3 Interleaving

1. Interleaving has a medium overall effect (g = 0.42, 59 studies) but large heterogeneity. It works best for visual categories (paintings) and is uncertain for words and expository text. The math effect is small. Source: [Brunmair and Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/). Evidence: meta-analysis. Confidence: about 80% for the numbers; about 50% that interleaving helps vocabulary specifically.
2. Practical reading: mixing question types and similar items (for example similar-sounding tones, similar kanji) is plausible; mixing unrelated words is not shown to help.

### 2.4 Comprehensible input, graded reading and listening

1. Extensive reading (large amounts of easy, self-chosen reading) improves reading comprehension and vocabulary. Jeon and Day 2016: about 0.54 (comprehension, 46 studies) and 0.47 (vocabulary, 17 studies). Nakanishi 2015 found 0.68 (comprehension) and 0.18 (vocabulary). Sources: [Jeon and Day 2016 and Nakanishi 2015 (via University of Hawaii repository)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899), [overview](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1078389.pdf). Evidence: two meta-analyses. Confidence: about 75%. Critics say some included studies did not reflect true extensive reading.
2. No source found tests Krashen's comprehension hypothesis directly (that input alone is enough). Popular claims that input alone suffices are not established. Confidence: about 70%.
3. Learning words from reading or listening alone is real but small: about 9 to 18 percent of target words learned per exposure episode, similar for reading, listening and reading while listening. Word-focused tasks gave larger gains than reading alone in one cited study (Laufer 2003, via secondary citation). Source: [Webb, Uchihara and Yanagisawa 2023, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C). Evidence: meta-analysis. Confidence: about 80%.
4. Coverage: about 98 percent known words for comfortable reading (8,000 to 9,000 word families for general texts, 6,000 to 7,000 for speech); 95 percent may be a minimum, and listening may work at lower coverage in some studies. Sources: [Nation 2006](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf), [Schmitt et al. 2015 replication](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_cobb_etal_2015.pdf). Evidence: corpus analysis and controlled studies (English). Confidence: about 70% (English figures; Chinese and Japanese differ).
5. Meaning for the app: texts and audio made only from words the learner already met, plus a few new ones, are supported. The app already writes stories and sentences from learned words (CLAUDE.md section 6).

### 2.5 Speaking, output and shadowing

1. No meta-analysis on shadowing was found in this search. The evidence for shadowing is therefore not confirmed here. Confidence that shadowing helps pronunciation or listening: about 50% (plausible, unverified). Source: search found only [HVPT meta-analyses](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/core-reader) and no shadowing study.
2. Evidence that forced speaking output itself causes learning (Swain's output hypothesis) is mixed; one source says there is a severe lack of data. Sources: [search results incl. output-based instruction meta-analyses](https://research.uaeu.ac.ae/en/publications/comprehensible-output-from-occurrence-to-acquisition-an-agenda-fo/). Evidence: reviews and small meta-analyses, mostly of grammar instruction. Confidence: about 55%.
3. Speaking practice has other reasons: it is a trip goal and it exercises retrieval of the production direction. The app already has typed and spoken answers.

### 2.6 Vocabulary in context versus in isolation

1. Both help. Word-focused practice (retrieval of the word with its meaning) is efficient; context adds meaning variety, collocations and listening exposure. The best-supported combination is a first meeting in a sentence or story, then retrieval practice with the word, then more meetings in new sentences. Sources: Webb et al. 2023 (above) and the Dunlosky summary. Evidence: meta-analysis and review (combined inference). Confidence: about 65%. This is an inference from two sources, not one direct comparison.
2. The app already shows each word with an example sentence and word breaks, and adds practice sentences per theme.

### 2.7 Mnemonics for kanji and hanzi

1. Evidence specific to kanji and hanzi mnemonics is thin. Studies found that mnemonics help when used meaningfully (components, meaning), and that heavy reliance on complex stories can crowd out the character's meaning. The Heisig method has no controlled trial found; its benefit claims come from promotional or secondary writing. Sources: [study collection (search result)](https://core.ac.uk/download/291344621.pdf), [Dunlosky summary rating keyword mnemonics low](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html). Evidence: small studies, review. Confidence: about 60%.
2. The app shows stroke order step by step and words using each kanji (js/kanji.js). Component or radical meaning is a lower-risk aid than long stories. Confidence that component-based hints help: about 55%.

### 2.8 Tones and pitch accent

1. Perception training with many different speakers (high variability phonetic training) improves second-language sound perception and pronunciation. Meta-analysis of 22 effect sizes: g = 0.77 on pronunciation (2024, via search summary); a larger meta-analysis of 79 studies exists. Sources: [Uchihara, Karas and Thomson, HVPT meta-analysis (SSLA)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/core-reader). Evidence: meta-analysis. Confidence: about 75% that perception training works; about 50% that many speakers beat one speaker (the 2021 talker-variability meta-analysis found mixed results: [JSLHR](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00181)).
2. Mandarin tone training: all trained groups improved in perception and production, with transfer to new voices; high versus low talker variability showed no clear generalisation benefit. Source: [PMC6690337](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6690337/). Evidence: controlled study. Confidence: about 70%.
3. Japanese pitch accent: six short sessions (30 minutes) of awareness and self-monitoring training improved perception and production for new words. Showing pitch height as a visual notation was the most robust condition; adding gesture gave no extra learning. Sources: [Hirano-Cook 2011](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/6eabc0f5-cb55-412e-85eb-c8e8c1659e34), [Hirata et al. 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/AB2195C963F348823C8175220F9F9EA1/core-reader). Evidence: controlled studies (small samples). Confidence: about 65%.
4. Text-to-speech voices on phones differ; they may not produce correct tones or pitch accent in every word. This is a risk for any training that relies on device voices (CLAUDE.md section 11 item 1). Confidence: about 70% (known limitation, no source retrieved).

### 2.9 Desirable difficulty versus frustration

1. Bjork's "desirable difficulties" (spacing, testing, interleaving) are supported by the sources above. The Bjork paper itself was not retrieved.
2. The "85 percent rule" (best accuracy about 85 percent) comes from simple two-choice learning models and animal studies. The lead author says it probably does not apply directly to students. Source: [University of Arizona news](https://news.arizona.edu/news/learning-optimized-when-we-fail-15-time) and the [paper record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v10y2019i1d10.1038_s41467-019-12552-4.html). Evidence: computational model and simple tasks. Confidence that it applies to vocabulary: about 30%. Use it only as a loose target (most answers correct, some errors), not as a rule.

### 2.10 Motivation without streak pressure

1. Evidence that streaks improve language learning (not only app use) was not found. Found: small surveys linking streaks to continued use, a thesis suggesting pressure can harm intrinsic motivation, and practitioner blogs. Sources: [Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/48993?show=full), [Yu-kai Chou (practitioner)](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-study/master-the-art-of-streak-design-for-short-term-engagement-and-long-term-success/). Evidence: student theses, opinion. Confidence that streaks do not help learning itself: about 60%.
2. No self-determination theory meta-analysis was retrieved in this search; do not cite SDT results from this report.
3. The app has no streaks, no leaderboards and no timers by design (CLAUDE.md section 3 rule 8).

### 2.11 Autistic and ADHD learners

1. Autistic learners: predictability and routine reduce anxiety; strengths-based, individualised approaches are recommended. Evidence is mostly guidance and scoping reviews; strategies are used inconsistently in schools. Sources: [NSW Department of Education](https://education.nsw.gov.au/campaigns/inclusive-practice-hub/all-resources/primary-resources/understanding-disability/autism/evidence-based-strategies), [Education Sciences 2023 scoping review](https://mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/6/575). Evidence: official guidance, scoping review. Confidence: about 65%.
2. Interest-based learning for autistic adolescents: the scoping review states the evidence is unclear. Most research is on young children. Confidence that it helps adults: about 45%.
3. ADHD: in one 2023 study, whole-text free recall beat section-by-section recall; neither beat restudy in that study. Earlier studies are mixed. Source: [Stern and Halamish 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10715433/). Evidence: controlled study. Confidence: about 50%.
4. Short sessions for ADHD: no controlled study found. This is practitioner advice only. Short sessions are still consistent with the spacing evidence (several short sessions are not worse than one long one) but that has not been shown for ADHD. Confidence: about 45%.

### 2.12 Weak claims to avoid

1. Learning styles (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic matching): lack of evidence for matching instruction to style. Source: [Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/). Evidence: review. Confidence: about 90%. Note: the owner is a "visual learner" as a preference; offering both text and audio is fine, but do not build the app on matching.
2. Fixed ratio rules for spacing, "85 percent rule" for students, and Heisig-style stories as proven: see 2.1, 2.9, 2.7.
3. Duolingo-style streaks as a learning method: see 2.10.

## 3. What the app does today (from code and CLAUDE.md)

1. Fixed review intervals by box: `INTERVAL=[0,1,2,4,8,16]` days (js/core.js line 22, `srsSet` line 161). A correct answer raises the box by 1 (maximum 5); a wrong answer resets to box 1 and the word is due again immediately. The same rule is copied in `onAnswer` (js/exercises.js), `kSet` (js/kanji.js) and `gradeCard` (js/listen.js).
2. Items due: `dueList` (js/core.js) lists words with `d <= now`. The Practise tab mixes up to 8 words, 4 kanji and 4 saved words in one session (`startMixed`, js/ui.js).
3. Retrieval: meaning, listen, type, speak, match, build sentence, listen and build, fill the blank, grammar choice, flashcards (js/exercises.js, js/exercise-types.js, js/listen.js).
4. Mistakes list for sentence and grammar questions, repeated once at the end of a session (misTrack, startMistakes).
5. Sentences session from learned themes (`startSentences`, js/ui.js).
6. Input: stories, podcast episodes, travel conversations, videos, Player with text modes and speed, word callout with reading (js/listen.js, js/sections.js, js/lessons.js).
7. Output: typed and spoken answers; Travel real-situation log.
8. Kanji with step-by-step strokes and fading furigana (js/kanji.js).
9. Reading aloud of new words and questions (setting autoRead).
10. No streaks, timers, leaderboards or animation. Sessions are small (review of 10, mixed of up to 16).
11. Not found in the code: any dedicated tone or pitch accent training (searches of js/ for tone, pitch and accent found only typing hints and speaker voice pitch); any shadowing mode; any per-word difficulty in scheduling; any "early review" rule.

## 4. Gaps

1. Scheduling ignores how hard a word is for this learner. A word answered wrong many times and an easy word follow the same table.
2. Practising early (Practise anyway, `startMixed(true)`) may raise the box of words that are not due. Early successes give less spacing benefit than successes at the due time. (Unconfirmed in the code; needs a test. Confidence about 60%.)
3. No tone training for Chinese and no pitch accent training for Japanese, although tone errors can change meaning, and the trip goal is real conversations.
4. No shadowing mode (repeat after the audio with the text, then without). Evidence for it is unconfirmed here.
5. Retrieval is mostly recognition (choose from options) in some exercises; recall (typing or saying) is stronger. The app has typing, but the mixed session may favour choice types.
6. No easy-input path with a known-word ratio (for example a "mostly known words" label on stories and podcasts).
7. No review in the opposite direction by default (meaning to word, production) for words saved from content.

## 5. Ranked proposals

Ranking uses: strength of evidence, benefit to this learner (trip, tones, kanji), effort, and design rule risk.

### Proposal 1: tone and pitch accent listening practice (Practise tab, new row)

1. Problem: no training for tones (Chinese) or pitch accent (Japanese).
2. Evidence: perception training meta-analyses (sections 2.8 items 1 to 3). Confidence about 70%.
3. Change: a short "Sounds" practice per language. Chinese: hear a syllable or word, choose the tone (or the matching pinyin with the tone mark) from 2 to 4 options, with the tone contour drawn as a static line shape. Japanese: hear a word, choose the pitch pattern shown as a static high and low line (pitch height notation was the most robust condition). Use words from the learner's learned list. Feedback uses the existing bottom panel. 10 items per session, no timer.
4. Screen: Practise tab row, sub-screen using the review template (registered in tests/phone/screens.js).
5. Effort: large. Needs pitch accent data for Japanese words (not in the content format today; a source such as a pitch accent dictionary is needed) and tone data is already in pinyin.
6. Design rules touched: rule 2 (one primary action), rule 4 (line icons only), rule 8 (no animation: contours are static). Open risk: device voices may speak tones or pitch wrongly (section 2.8 item 4); this is a question for the owner (section 6).

### Proposal 2: adaptive review interval using the answer history (no new tab)

1. Problem: fixed table; no difficulty.
2. Evidence: adaptive models predict recall better (section 2.1 items 5 and 6). Confidence about 65% for benefit; about 50% that the benefit is large.
3. Change, small version: store per word a count of wrong answers (`l` for lapses) and use a shorter step after 2 or more lapses (for example the interval is multiplied by 0.5 when lapses are 2 or more, and by 1.0 otherwise). Count also how late the answer was: if a word is answered after its due date, keep the box. Medium version: replace the table with a simple FSRS-like stability value and a "target retention" setting (default 90 percent) in Settings. Keep the visible text literal: "Words you find hard come back sooner."
4. Screen: no new screen. Settings may gain one row for the medium version.
5. Effort: small for the lapse count (touches `srsSet`, `onAnswer`, `kSet`, `gradeCard`, `normalize`, `stateRows`, `mergeRemote`, `supabase.sql` columns or a field inside existing data; data-engineer subagent). Medium for an FSRS-like model.
6. Design rules touched: none visible. Rule from section 5: a new progress field must be added to `stateRows`, `mergeRemote` and `normalize()`.

### Proposal 3: do not advance the box for answers given before the due time

1. Problem: possible early-practice inflation of intervals (gap 2).
2. Evidence: spacing meta-analysis (the gap should grow with the retention goal, section 2.1 items 1 and 2). Confidence about 60%.
3. Change: in `srsSet` and the three copies, if the word is not yet due, record the answer but keep the same box and due date when correct (and still reset when wrong). Confirm first with a test that "Practise anyway" can touch not-due words.
4. Screen: none.
5. Effort: small.
6. Design rules touched: none. This also removes four copies of the same rule if refactored into one function (optional).

### Proposal 4: "mostly known words" label and a Listen filter for easy input

1. Problem: no way to choose input at the right level.
2. Evidence: extensive reading and coverage studies (section 2.4 items 1 and 4). Confidence about 65%.
3. Change: compute for each story, podcast and travel conversation the share of words the learner has learned (from `srs` and `lc`). Show it as a literal line on the Listen tile, for example "You know 96 of 100 words." Add a filter "Easy for me" (at least 95 percent known) in the existing Listen filter. No ranking, no scores.
4. Screen: Listen tab tiles (`homeListen`).
5. Effort: medium (a word-coverage function over sentence tokens; tokens already exist in stories).
6. Design rules touched: rule 1 (stays inside Listen), rule 8 (no scores or competition). Percent shown as a plain fact.

### Proposal 5: shadowing step in the Player (optional, off by default)

1. Problem: no practice repeating sentences aloud with the model audio.
2. Evidence: not confirmed in this search (section 2.5). Confidence about 50%. Low risk, but the benefit is unproven here.
3. Change: in the Player and in the lesson example, a "Say it with the voice" step: the line is played, then the learner says it (microphone optional; if no recognition, no scoring). No timer; the learner taps Next. If the owner approves, run a second research pass on shadowing before building.
4. Screen: the Player (`view.s="player"`), inside the Listen tab.
5. Effort: medium.
6. Design rules touched: rule 8 (no auto-advance: the learner taps Next), rule 2 (one primary action).

### Lower ranked ideas

6. Prefer typed or spoken recall over choice in the mixed session for words in box 3 or higher (small, evidence 2.2 item 2, confidence about 70%, effort small).
7. Reverse-direction questions (meaning to word) for saved words (small to medium, evidence 2.2, confidence about 60%).
8. Component or radical meaning hint on kanji cards instead of stories (medium, evidence 2.7, confidence about 55%, needs data from KANJIDIC2 or similar).
9. An end-of-session literal summary with the count of answers right this week and the next due date instead of any streak (small; no evidence it improves learning; the owner decides).

## 6. Open questions for the owner

1. For tone and pitch accent practice (Proposal 1): do you accept that your phone voices may speak a tone or pitch pattern wrongly in some words, or do you want recorded audio from a source you approve for these exercises?
2. For Proposal 2: do you prefer the small version (lapse count, easy to explain) or a target retention setting (more control, one more setting)?
3. For Proposal 5: do you want a second research pass on shadowing before any design?
4. Does a "known words" percentage on Listen tiles (Proposal 4) count as pressure for you, or as useful information?
5. Is the 16-item mixed session (about 6 minutes) the right size, or should there be a shorter option? No evidence was found for an exact length for autistic or ADHD learners.

## 7. Limits of this research

1. About 25 searches were run; most retrieved summaries or abstracts, not full texts. Primary papers were not opened in full.
2. Not retrieved: the FSRS srs-benchmark results, Bjork 1994, any shadowing meta-analysis, any self-determination theory meta-analysis, Heisig method trials, Wang et al. 1999.
3. Most vocabulary coverage and testing effect numbers come from English or general education studies. Effects for Chinese and Japanese learners are expected to be similar but are not shown here.
